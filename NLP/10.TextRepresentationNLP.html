Chapter 10: Fundamentals of Text Representation in NLP

10.1. Introduction: Why We Need Text Representation

Natural Language Processing (NLP) seeks to enable machines to understand and work with human language. However, computers process numbers, not words. To bridge this gap, we need text representation—a way to convert language into numerical data while retaining as much meaning and structure as possible.

10.2. Why Text Must Be Converted to Numbers

Most machine learning algorithms expect numerical input in the form of vectors or matrices. So a natural language modelling technique like Word Embedding is used to map words or phrases from a vocabulary to a corresponding vector of real numbers. As well as being amenable to processing by ML algorithms, this vector representation has two important and advantageous properties:

  • Dimensionality Reduction - it is a more efficient representation
  • Contextual Similarity - Preserve semantic meaning, it is a more expressive representation"

10.3. Foundational Techniques in Text Representation

10.3.1 One-Hot Encoding

Each word is represented by a binary vector with one element set to 1 and the rest 0.

Example:

Sentences:

  1. I love NLP
  2. NLP loves me

Vocabulary: ["I", "love", "NLP", "loves", "me"]

WordIndex

love1

loves3
me4

Sentence 1: "I love NLP"

WordOne-Hot Vector
I[1, 0, 0, 0, 0]
love[0, 1, 0, 0, 0]
NLP[0, 0, 1, 0, 0]

Sentence 2: "NLP loves me"

WordOne-Hot Vector
NLP[0, 0, 1, 0, 0]
loves[0, 0, 0, 1, 0]
me[0, 0, 0, 0, 1]

✅ Easy to implement
❌ No similarity captured; high dimensionality

10.3.2 Count Vectorizer (Bag-of-Words)

Represents documents by counting how many times each word appears, ignoring grammar and order.

Example:

Sentences:

  1. I love NLP
  2. NLP loves me

Vocabulary: ["i", "love", "nlp", "loves", "me"]

Word Sentence 1 ("I love NLP") Sentence 2 ("NLP loves me")
i10
love10
nlp11
loves01
me01

Vector Representation

  • Sentence 1: [1, 1, 1, 0, 0]
  • Sentence 2: [0, 0, 1, 1, 1]

This representation is called the Bag-of-Words model, where we count the number of times each word appears in a document.

✅ Simple and effective
❌ Ignores order and meaning

10.3.3 N-grams

Captures short sequences of adjacent words to include some sense of context.

Example:

Sentences:

  1. I love NLP
  2. NLP loves me

Step 1: Extracted Bigrams

  • I love NLP → "I love", "love NLP"
  • NLP loves me → "NLP loves", "loves me"

Step 2: Bigram Vocabulary

["I love", "love NLP", "NLP loves", "loves me"]

Step 3: Vector Representation

Bigram Sentence 1 ("I love NLP") Sentence 2 ("NLP loves me")
I love10
love NLP10
NLP loves01
loves me01

Final Vectors

  • Sentence 1: [1, 1, 0, 0]
  • Sentence 2: [0, 0, 1, 1]

This representation captures the sequence of two consecutive words, making it more informative than single-word models.

✅ Captures local patterns
❌ Rapid vocabulary growth with larger 'n'

10.3.4 TF-IDF (Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency)

Enhances Count Vectorizer by weighing words based on importance.

  • TF (Term Frequency): Frequency of the word in the document
  • IDF (Inverse Document Frequency): Rarity of the word across documents

Example:

Sentences:

  1. I love NLP
  2. NLP loves me

Step 1: Vocabulary

["I", "love", "NLP", "loves", "me"]

Step 2: Term Frequency (TF)

Word Sentence 1 Sentence 2
I1/30
love1/30
NLP1/31/3
loves01/3
me01/3

Step 3: Inverse Document Frequency (IDF)

Total documents (N) = 2

Word df(w) IDF = ln(2 / df(w))
I10.693
love10.693
NLP20
loves10.693
me10.693

Step 4: TF-IDF = TF × IDF

Word Sentence 1 (TF-IDF) Sentence 2 (TF-IDF)
I0.2310
love0.2310
NLP0.00.0
loves00.231
me00.231

Final TF-IDF Vectors

  • Sentence 1: [0.231, 0.231, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0]
  • Sentence 2: [0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.231, 0.231]

TF-IDF emphasizes important terms in each sentence and reduces the impact of common words like "NLP", which appears in both.

✅ Downplays common words
❌ Still sparse; doesn’t capture meaning

10.4. Dense Representations: Word Embeddings

Traditional methods are sparse and miss meaning. Word embeddings solve this by mapping words into low-dimensional, dense vectors.

Word embeddings are dense, low-dimensional vector representations of words that capture semantic relationships. Unlike sparse vectors from One-Hot or BoW, embeddings learn patterns and context from large text data.

10.4.1 Word Embeddings (Concept)

Words are placed in a vector space where similar words are close together.

Example:

Word Vector
king[0.8, 0.2, 0.6]
queen[0.9, 0.1, 0.7]
man[0.7, 0.3, 0.5]
woman[0.9, 0.1, 0.6]

Vector Arithmetic Analogy

king - man + woman ≈ queen

[0.8, 0.2, 0.6] 
- [0.7, 0.3, 0.5] 
+ [0.9, 0.1, 0.6] 
= [1.0, 0.0, 0.7] ≈ queen → [0.9, 0.1, 0.7]

Python Example using Gensim Word2Vec

from gensim.models import Word2Vec

sentences = [
    ["king", "queen", "man", "woman"],
    ["paris", "france", "rome", "italy"],
    ["apple", "banana", "orange", "fruit"],
]

model = Word2Vec(sentences, vector_size=3, window=2, min_count=1, sg=1)

print("Vector for 'king':", model.wv['king'])
print("Similar to 'king':", model.wv.most_similar('king'))
print("king - man + woman ≈", model.wv.most_similar(positive=['king', 'woman'], negative=['man']))
✅ Low dimensional
❌ Needs training or pre-trained models

10.6. Conclusion: From Tokens to Meaning

Text representation has evolved from basic binary and count-based models to sophisticated dense embeddings. While foundational techniques like One-Hot and TF-IDF are still useful in some scenarios, modern NLP systems increasingly rely on word embeddings to unlock semantic understanding.

Techniques like GloVe serve as stepping stones to even more advanced methods like contextual embeddings (ELMo, BERT, GPT models), where a word’s vector changes depending on context. Understanding the basics of text representation is essential for building intelligent language systems.
